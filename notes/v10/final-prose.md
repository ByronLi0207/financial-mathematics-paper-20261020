## Abstract

Dealer peak-position forecasts improve with conditioning only in some samples and under specific benchmarks. Matched FX regression lowers loss by 14.8% and 16.6% across two retrospective periods. Against the original shorter rolling reference, earlier loss rises by 3.2%; the earlier Treasury short-history comparison rises by 144.3%. We compare matched forecasts, independent reporting systems and limit-cost rankings. Historical rules beat expanded regression in 34 of 48 additional weekly comparisons. In 17 related CFTC dealer-category futures series, F or I beats regression in 29 of 34 comparisons. Regression's median excess losses are 19.3% and 10.3% across the two histories. The 90% model confidence set retains matched historical rules in 90 of 98 comparisons, usually alongside alternatives. On fixed calibrated paths, allowing stronger penalties and I reduces short-history median losses from 116.2% and 121.4% to 0.2% and -0.2%. Parameter switching misses the registered additional-loss threshold. Limit-cost rankings change with reserve, breach-frequency and exceedance charges. Peak-limit validation therefore needs matched references, complete tuning procedures and separate error-cost components.

## Research questions

RQ1 asks whether conditional quantile methods improve sampled peak forecasts with matched histories and update schedules.

RQ2 asks whether those gains extend across frequencies and independently reported dealer-position aggregates.

RQ3 asks whether score gains imply lower limit costs under reserve, breach and exceedance preferences.

## Contributions

First, matched FX gains recur, but the original-reference direction changes. Regression lowers matched loss by 14.8% and 16.6% (Table 3). Earlier loss rises by 3.2% against the original rolling reference. Delayed calibration retains the later matched gain with 329 versus 328 breaches.

Second, historical benchmarks remain competitive across reporting systems, and tuning is consequential. F or I beats expanded regression in 29/34 CFTC comparisons (Table 5). Regression's median excess loss is 19.3% at history 52 and 10.3% at 260. Matched F/I enters 90/98 model confidence sets, but is the sole method in only 2. In paired simulations, allowing stronger penalties and I changes constant/switching short-history medians to 0.2%/-0.2%. Both improve over the original menu; the registered switching increment is unsupported.

Third, lower score and lower limit cost have different preference regions. At c = 1, across the displayed coefficient grid, expanded regression is cheaper in 1–32 of 34 CFTC comparisons. Figure 4 retains the complete regions for all source groups. Reserve amounts, breach counts and exceedance amounts determine different parts of the ranking.

## Conclusion

RQ1 has a reference-dependent answer. Hourly FX regression lowers matched loss by 14.8% and 16.6% across periods. Delayed calibration retains the later gain with 329 versus 328 breaches. Earlier loss is 3.2% higher against the original shorter rolling reference; that direction fails to recur. The earlier Treasury short-history comparison raises loss by 144.3%.

For RQ2, historical rules remain strong across related weekly categories, same-record daily aggregates and the independent CFTC reporting system. F or I beats expanded regression in 29/34 CFTC comparisons. Regression's median excess losses are 19.3%/10.3% at the two histories. Matched F/I survives in 90/98 model confidence sets, usually alongside other methods. Expanded menus reduce weekly losses, but short-sample validation still fails to protect the historical benchmark consistently. With stronger penalties and I, paired simulations reduce constant/switching short-history medians to 0.2%/-0.2%; the registered switching increment is unsupported. The empirical low-frequency mechanism remains unidentified.

For RQ3, score gains translate into lower limit costs only in parts of the coefficient grid. At c = 1, CFTC regression is cheaper in 1–32 of 34 comparisons as reserve and breach charges vary. Limit validation should match histories and schedules, retain historical controls, and report reserve amounts, breach counts and excess amounts together.
