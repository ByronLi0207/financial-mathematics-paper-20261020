## 1. Constructed populations and financial error calibration

**Campbell R. Harvey and Yan Liu (2020), “False (and Missed) Discoveries in Financial Economics.” The Journal of Finance 75(5), 2503–2553, October 2020.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12951); [actual author-hosted published copy](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P143_False_and_missed.pdf). Actual version/read scope appears in the table below.

**Source synthesis — 149 words.**

<!-- SYNTHESIS_1_START -->
Harvey and Liu use a double bootstrap to evaluate financial false and missed discoveries. An assumed positive-effect fraction guides outer ranking and recentering of an original panel into hypothetical populations. Inner resampling then evaluates testing decisions against the assigned effects. Known labels belong to those constructed panels, not to observed market skill. The missed-discovery denominator counts declared negatives, distinguishing false omission from the conventional missed fraction among true alternatives. Their error ratio is an economic heuristic rather than a complete allocation objective. Common-period resampling retains strategy and factor alignment, but does not by itself establish arbitrary serial-dependence or stopping-time validity. Fixed-cutoff calibration averages over outer configurations conditional on the assumed effect fraction. Existing applications include strategy panels, anomalies and mutual-fund joint tests. Publication selection, weak sparse effects and data restrictions qualify the findings. Selected published passages were read; supplementary validation, executable implementation, observations and present reuse permissions remain unverified.

## 2. Directional discoveries, subsequent persistence and costs

**Pierre Bajgrowicz and Olivier Scaillet (2012), “Technical trading revisited: False discoveries, persistence tests, and transaction costs.” Journal of Financial Economics 106(3), 473–491, December 2012.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.06.001); [actual accepted-manuscript institutional record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:85618). An exact first-online day was not independently established.

**Source synthesis — 152 words.**

<!-- SYNTHESIS_2_START -->
Bajgrowicz and Scaillet apply directional false-discovery estimation to technical trading, extending an existing mutual-fund method. Their accepted manuscript combines rule screening, equal-weight signal aggregation, monthly persistence assessment and transaction costs within selection. The specified family contains 7846 historical rule variants. Past signals and following-month evaluation distinguish a tradable selection exercise from identifying winners retrospectively within a historical interval. Selecting cost-adjusted candidates can differ from deducting costs only after choosing gross winners. Individual performance p-values use stationary resampling, while cross-rule proportion estimation invokes separate weak-dependence arguments and simulations. Temporal blocks and dependence across the rule family are different approximation layers. Estimated directional FDR is not observed true FDP for a selected portfolio. Reported historical persistence and net-performance findings remain specific to the study's benchmark, market and execution assumptions. This is established financial screening with costs. Selected accepted-body methods and appendices were read; final-version equivalence, external proofs, programs, observations and current permissions remain unverified.

## 3. A searched rule family and different later-period exercises

**Ryan Sullivan, Allan Timmermann and Halbert White (1999), “Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap.” The Journal of Finance 54(5), 1647–1691, October 1999.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00163). Actual source: [LSE Financial Markets Group Discussion Paper 303, October 1998](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/119144/1/dp303.pdf), marked forthcoming; final-journal equivalence is unverified.

**Source synthesis — 147 words.**

<!-- SYNTHESIS_3_START -->
The October 1998 Sullivan–Timmermann–White draft applies a reality-check bootstrap to a defined technical-rule universe. Its global null asks whether any candidate has positive expected performance relative to a benchmark. A rejection addresses existence within that family, rather than identifying every superior rule or controlling individual discoveries. Common resampling indices and centered joint performance statistics retain cross-rule alignment under the required external asymptotic conditions. The correction does not cover every omitted specification or historical research choice. The draft distinguishes evaluating a rule selected by 1986, selecting a winner again within later observations and following the previous day's cumulative-wealth leader. These are different later-period selection targets. It also discusses incomplete historical dividends, transaction costs and a separate futures comparison. Reported significance is not a complete economic-value or arbitrary-stopping guarantee. Selected original-draft methods and appendices were read; final-edition equivalence, external conditions, executable rule details, observations and redistribution permissions remain unverified.

## 4. Joint resampling for asymptotic FDR

**Joseph P. Romano, Azeem M. Shaikh and Michael Wolf (2008), “Control of the false discovery rate under dependence using the bootstrap and subsampling.” TEST 17(3), 417–442, November 2008.** First online **30 October 2008**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11749-008-0126-6); [actual author-hosted formal copy](https://home.uchicago.edu/amshaikh/webfiles/fdr.pdf).

**Source synthesis — 148 words.**

<!-- SYNTHESIS_4_START -->
Romano, Shaikh and Wolf develop stepdown procedures using joint resampling information for asymptotic FDR control. The bootstrap theorem requires consistent joint approximation, stable positive standardizers and a true-null limiting law with continuous marginals, connected support and exchangeability. Marginally accurate tests alone do not establish those conditions. Subsampling removes exchangeability through a different construction, while retaining limiting-law and size/rate assumptions. Its formal theorem starts with independent observations; stationary consecutive-block extensions refer to external theory. The family is fixed as sample size grows, and the proofs separate fixed alternatives from bounded true-null statistics. These pointwise asymptotic statements are neither finite-sample exact nor calendar-time monitoring guarantees. The hedge-fund application already tests mean returns against a common risk-free benchmark using circular-block resampling. That estimand differs from factor alpha and subsequent portfolio profitability. Selected formal methods and proof routes were read; external primitive conditions, implementation, original observations and present reuse permissions remain unverified.

## 5. Generalized error targets and distinct selection questions

**Joseph P. Romano, Azeem M. Shaikh and Michael Wolf (2008), “Formalized Data Snooping Based on Generalized Error Rates.” Econometric Theory 24(2), 404–447, April 2008.** First online **30 November 2007**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266466608080171); [actual author-hosted formal copy](https://home.uchicago.edu/amshaikh/webfiles/snoop.pdf).

**Source synthesis — 150 words.**

<!-- SYNTHESIS_5_START -->
This econometric article distinguishes generalized familywise error, expected FDP and the probability that FDP exceeds a tolerance. Its resampling k-StepM and FDP-StepM constructions use a fixed hypothesis family, joint limiting distributions and bootstrap consistency; studentized versions add standard-error conditions. Central validity arguments refer to earlier generalized-error theory. Increasing k operates on one observed sample and is not a rule for stopping future market observation. The source also separates beating a benchmark, trimming weak forecasts, selecting coefficients and covering a best model. These decisions require different guarantees. Its financial application compares net-fee hedge-fund log returns with a risk-free benchmark, rather than treating every performance measure as factor alpha. Joint resampling, computational subset shortcuts and fitted-law block calibration are established components. Historical rejection counts do not establish future persistence or known realized FDP. Selected formal methods, application and appendix were read; source programs, observations, full external assumptions and present permissions remain unverified.
<!-- SYNTHESIS_5_END -->

## Original crosswalk of claims

| Question | Distinction retained across the readings |
|---|---|
| What does a historical discovery establish? | A global existence rejection, an individual selection, a population proportion estimate and a best-model confidence set are different claims. |
| Where are true labels known? | Assigned effects in a constructed bootstrap panel permit error counting there; market skill labels remain unobserved. |
| What persists into the future? | A frozen earlier winner, repeated later selection and a predictable updating rule have different selection and evaluation histories. |
| Which economic target is tested? | Relative payoff means, Sharpe measures and factor alpha retain their own benchmark and cost definitions; multiplicity adjustment does not equate them. |
| Where do costs enter? | Selecting on gross performance and later subtracting costs differs from selecting cost-adjusted candidates before evaluation. |
| Which error is controlled? | Expected FDP, FDP exceedance and k-or-more false rejections have different probability statements and cannot be ordered by discovery counts alone. |
| What does dependence correction assume? | The already-read [Benjamini–Yekutieli foundation](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1013699998) adjusts dependence among valid p-values; it does not establish those inputs' sampling validity. |
| When is a stepdown reduction finite? | The already-read [Romano–Wolf foundation](https://doi.org/10.1214/009053606000001622) distinguishes a finite known-law monotonicity reduction from asymptotic bootstrap calibration and calendar-time stopping. |

These are existing financial methods and inferential distinctions. Adopting them, changing a market series or adding another historical selection date is not by itself a methodological contribution. No testing target, family, benchmark, data source, holding period or experiment is selected here.
