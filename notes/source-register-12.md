## 1. A working likelihood for joint tail forecasting

**James W. Taylor (2019), “Forecasting Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall Using a Semiparametric Approach Based on the Asymmetric Laplace Distribution.” Journal of Business & Economic Statistics 37(1), 121–133.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.2017.1281815), [Oxford author copy](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~mast0315/VaRESALD.pdf).

Actual reading: selected methods, evaluation discussion and conclusion in the 32-page author copy; key formula and optimization-count pages were visually checked. Online publication is dated 19 May 2017, distinct from the 2019 issue. The formal journal layout and supplement were not independently read. Source SHA-256: `9acb065ea7040cee9809406f0389697ee156aee5a3b5e0dcdf3ff7b43e2b476d`.

Taylor develops dynamic joint quantile and expected-shortfall forecasts using an asymmetric-Laplace working objective. Its interpretation does not require the true return distribution to be asymmetric Laplace. The conditional mean, negative-tail score domain and admissible VaR–ES ordering are substantive parts of the construction. Removing a candidate-dependent return-over-ES term requires the appropriate conditional-centering and integrability argument; unconditional sample centering alone does not establish it. The paper already compares rolling daily tail forecasts for three stock indices against historical simulation, volatility-based and quantile-based alternatives. Its reported score comparisons, calibration diagnostics and crisis sensitivity exercises answer different questions. The source's large starting-value searches are historical implementation choices, rather than measured costs or an adopted computational plan here. This is mature financial forecasting methodology. Another return series, an asymmetric-Laplace label or a familiar joint score alone would not establish a new method. The source's empirical results and software were not reproduced, and current source-code availability and raw-data redistribution rights were not established.

## 2. Conditional joint quantile–ES regression

**Timo Dimitriadis and Sebastian Bayer (2019), “A joint quantile and expected shortfall regression framework.” Electronic Journal of Statistics 13(1), 1823–1871.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1214/19-EJS1560), [actual arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02213v3).

Actual reading: selected methods, assumptions, internal proofs and IBM application from the 31-page v3 submitted 8 August 2017, whose cover says 07.08.2017. Formal 2019 publication metadata were verified, but its 49-page journal body was not obtained. Detailed theorem claims concern v3. Source SHA-256: `7f9f966468724c4721d6594f2f6709a3af99f33e1a2dd388fbac4e616e360a36`.

Dimitriadis and Bayer estimate conditional linear quantile and ES equations jointly with consistent losses. The read preprint develops M- and Z-estimator asymptotics under correctly specified conditional functional models, iid observation pairs, positive conditional density near the quantile, full-rank regressors and theorem-specific moment conditions. Conditional heteroskedasticity is compatible with these assumptions; generic serial dependence is not silently included. Covariance estimation requires additional density and truncated-variance quantities. Its “nid” label allows residual variation with covariates and does not mean a time-series HAC theorem. The existing IBM application uses lagged realized volatility and rolling estimation, with pointwise score-comparison bands. That application does not change the stated sampling assumptions or establish simultaneous dominance over every score threshold. Author-linked package and replication documentation were inspected, but no program or TAQ observations were acquired. This is established prior work for joint financial tail regression, while misspecification, dependent observations and boundary-constrained combination weights require their own applicable theory.

## 3. Directly combining VaR and ES forecasts

**James W. Taylor (2020), “Forecast Combinations for Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall.” International Journal of Forecasting 36(2), 428–441.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijforecast.2019.05.014), [Oxford author copy](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~mast0315/CombiningVaRES_IJF.pdf).

Actual reading: selected score definitions, combination equations, rolling chronology, evaluation and conclusion in the 36-page author copy. Its PDF timestamp is April 2022 without an explicit author revision label; this is not identified as a new 2022 paper. Formal journal layout and online appendix were not separately read. Source SHA-256: `cd4606cba14b230ab02173c364e96dcace3986c2e7d6cf1a0c18f18406f80d35`.

Taylor combines tail-forecast pairs directly, including forecasts that do not supply a complete density. Minimum-score combining uses separate simplex weights for quantiles and ES-minus-quantile gaps. Relative-score combining applies common exponential historical-score weights to both coordinates, with one tuned parameter. Valid component pairs retain their ordering under these constructions. This deterministic property does not imply calibration or score dominance. The financial study already uses five stock indices, rolling component estimation, a separate rolling combination window and a final evaluation period. Calibration diagnostics, joint-score skill measures and model confidence sets remain distinct, and the source explicitly omits parameter-estimation uncertainty from calibration testing. Reported historical success is not a finite-sample or anytime aggregation theorem. Exact scoring formulas and their common action domains matter; names alone do not identify equivalent objectives. Direct tail-pair averaging also differs from taking VaR and ES after mixing CDFs. Another index, common convex weights or a tail-gap construction alone is therefore insufficient evidence of novelty. No observations, implementation or reported numerical results were reproduced.

## 4. Recent depth-based aggregation and an existing downside-risk application

**James W. Taylor (2026), “Probabilistic Forecast Aggregation with Statistical Depth.” European Journal of Operational Research 328(2), 460–476.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2025.06.028), [Oxford author copy](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~mast0315/CombiningDepth_EJOR.pdf).

Actual reading: selected interval/CDF methods, information requirements, empirical comparisons, regularization interpretation and growth-at-risk application in the 35-page author copy. Its production metadata is dated 16 December 2025; the indexed issue date is 16 January 2026. Exact first-online date was not verified. Supplement, code and datasets were not read. Source SHA-256: `61bf6aaeef72fe68dfe5a94c81ef405c74109e18ce986ae90eed9c8cb9b4f817`.

Taylor orders interval forecasts using bivariate statistical depth and whole predictive distributions using functional depth of their quantile functions. Entire CDFs can then be selected, trimmed or linearly pooled. Depth measures centrality, rather than probability, coverage or a confidence level. Parameter-free deepest aggregation uses the current pool; tuned trimming and width-weighted rules still need historical forecast pools and outcomes, even without complete individual-forecaster histories. Evaluation distinguishes interval score, CRPS, width, unconditional coverage and PIT diagnostics. Four quarterly surveys can forecast the same annual realization, so they are not four independent annual outcomes. Reported ECB comparisons include negative skill and strong simple-average or width-weighted benchmarks. Crucially, the article already applies bivariate depth to paired 5% and 10% GDP growth-at-risk forecasts. Thus applying depth to downside quantile aggregation is already prior work. Financial VaR–ES combinations also have the direct predecessor above. No universal calibration, outperformance or finite-sample guarantee is inferred from the depth construction or its regularized-pool interpretation.

## 5. Upgrade from abstract screening: risk-tuned regression

**R. Tyrrell Rockafellar, Stan Uryasev and Michael Zabarankin (2008), “Risk Tuning with Generalized Linear Regression.” Mathematics of Operations Research 33(3), 712–729.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.1080.0313), [official author publication list, entry 58](https://uryasev.github.io/publications/).

Register 11 recorded abstract/metadata screening. This update adds selected reading of the actual 18-page typeset article, especially error projection, regression decomposition and the nonnegative-portfolio factor bounds. It does not claim a full external-proof audit or an empirical replication. The separate Washington author file has 24 pages and was used for identity/version inspection. Formal source SHA-256: `a2771fcff4d1f024474f2a26a994e7c8c80ac0bec698d3f4a85afb9698e8d8f8`.

Rockafellar, Uryasev and Zabarankin adapt linear approximation to positively homogeneous convex error functionals. Minimizing over constant shifts yields a deviation measure and a potentially interval-valued statistic. Slopes minimize residual deviation, while the intercept centers the associated statistic. This is population approximation under a given joint law, not a generalized linear model with an exponential-family link. The financial factor application derives risk/deviation approximation bounds for constant nonnegative portfolio exposures; residual-risk premises and gain/loss orientation matter. These are analytical bounds under the same law. Exact finite-scenario optimization does not create statistical confidence about an unknown population. General empirical sampling asymptotics are explicitly deferred in the source. Its framework already covers choosing regression criteria for a financial risk purpose, including mixed-quantile constructions. The original functional assumptions, exposure restrictions and estimation uncertainty must therefore be examined before a transfer. No factor estimates, trading strategy, financial observations or runtime results were produced here.
