## Abstract

Inventory monitoring concerns the largest position reached during a horizon as well as its endpoint. Using an hourly FX client-flow position record and weekly Treasury-bill aggregates, we compare historical rules and conditional quantiles on matched completed-label histories and update schedules. Long-history regression reduces six-step FX pinball loss by 12.7%, while its original Treasury counterpart increases loss by 50.8%. Expanding the Treasury penalty menu reduces that excess to 3.4%, approaching an intercept-only empirical-quantile reference while preserving the FX gain. Across 12 additional archived weekly series, expansion reduces median excess loss from 92.6% to 9.2%, but regression still loses in 34 of 48 cells. Restrictive tuning can therefore explain substantial deterioration without explaining it all. Lower mean score coexists with more breaches, and neither primary comparison dominates across all consistent quantile scores. The analyses are retrospective: FX outcomes were previously inspected, Treasury is replayed by economic date from a downloaded vintage, and the panel shares dealer reporting and overlapping positions.

## Introduction contribution paragraph

Our empirical contribution has three parts. First, we compare established forecasts of a two-sided sampled peak using matched completed-label histories, extending the endpoint question of Wisniewski et al. (2020) (Tables 3–4). Second, we find that restrictive regularization explains much of the Treasury regression's deterioration: a shared wider penalty menu brings its main excess loss down to 3.4%, close to the intercept-only reference. The additional weekly panel shows substantial remaining losses. At the long FX history, the richer predictor set retains a 10.9% gain over the four-predictor procedure under the same menu (Section 6.3); this compares tuning procedures and does not isolate individual lag or calendar effects. Third, we connect the scoring perspective of Gneiting (2011); Ehm et al. (2016) to joint error-amount and breach evidence, showing that the mean-score ranking need not persist across all consistent scores (Table 8 and Appendix A.6).

## Conclusion

Historical rules are demanding benchmarks for forecasts of sampled position peaks. In the hourly FX record, long-history regression lowers the six-step primary pinball loss by 12.7%, although breaches increase. In the weekly Treasury record, expanding the ridge menu reduces the original regression's excess loss from 50.8% to 3.4%, close to an intercept-only empirical-quantile reference. Across the additional weekly panel, the corresponding median excess falls from 92.6% to 9.2%, yet 34 of 48 cells still lose to historical headroom. Stronger shrinkage explains substantial deterioration without eliminating it. These findings favor a selection menu containing both an intercept-only option and the exact historical benchmark, accompanied by joint reporting of underprediction, overprediction and breaches. Including a benchmark can protect the minimum validation score; it cannot guarantee evaluation performance. The surviving FX gain concerns a richer predictor procedure, without identifying a causal contribution from individual predictors. Neither primary uniformly dominates across all consistent quantile scores. Prior inspection, overlapping targets, downloaded-vintage Treasury observations and the panel's shared reporting system limit inference. The evidence concerns these retrospective forecasting exercises and does not establish gains from changing actual inventory or funding decisions.
