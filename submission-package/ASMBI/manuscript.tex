\title{Hard to Beat: Historical Rules versus Conditional Quantile Models for Peak Net-Position Limits}
\author{[AUTHOR INFORMATION TO BE COMPLETED]}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}Position limits must cover the largest absolute position reached during a review period, since an acceptable endpoint can conceal an earlier breach. We retrospectively compare historical and conditional quantile forecasts using hourly positions from one foreign exchange (FX) market maker and weekly dealer aggregates from the New York Fed and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Historical rules are hard to beat: regression improves FX peak forecasts with long training histories, but the measured gain depends on the historical reference. In the weekly comparisons, short training or validation histories favor historical rules; longer validation narrows median loss gaps, while large individual losses remain. Lower forecast scores need not imply lower limit costs, so model validation should report scores, breach counts and costs together.\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Peak net positions; Quantile forecasting; Historical benchmarks; Forecast comparison; Calibration.\par\medskip
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Dealer position limits concern the largest exposure reached before a review horizon ends, since an endpoint within the limit can conceal an earlier breach (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). We therefore compare historical and conditional forecasts of the sampled absolute-position peak, using the same target for every method.

Historical headroom combines the current magnitude with completed past peak behavior, while conditional models estimate relationships from finite samples. A matched comparison gives every method the same completed past outcomes, training-window length and model update times.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.94\linewidth]{figures/v9-endpoint-peak-example.pdf}
\caption{A selected Treasury-bill path that returns within the reported historical limit at its endpoint after an intervening breach. The rule chooses the earliest eligible original evaluation window. Endpoint containment can conceal a peak breach.}\label{fig:example}
\end{figure}

Our evidence covers hourly foreign exchange (FX) positions, their daily aggregates, New York Fed categories and Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) dealer-category futures positions. The two official weekly reporting systems describe different aggregate exposures, so their comparisons retain separate reference procedures and separate exposure units.

Three questions organize the evaluation.

\textbf{RQ1} asks whether conditional quantile methods improve sampled peak forecasts in matched comparisons.

\textbf{RQ2} asks whether those gains extend across frequencies and independently reported dealer-position aggregates.

\textbf{RQ3} asks whether score gains imply lower limit costs under reserve, breach and exceedance preferences.

The paper makes three empirical findings.

\textit{First, matched FX gains recur across periods but remain reference-dependent.} Long-history regression lowers matched loss by 14.8\% and 16.6\%, while its ranking against the shorter rolling rule changes (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). The original-period learning curve improves with more history before surrendering part of the gain at the longest history (Figure~\ref{fig:v11-history}). Matching peak responses, completed labels and refit schedules makes these comparisons interpretable without isolating causal feature effects.

\textit{Second, longer validation improves weekly full regression medians while leaving large losses on individual tasks.} Historical controls remain competitive in both weekly reporting systems, while extended validation reduces full regression medians (Section~\ref{sec:v12-low-frequency}). The CFTC short-history median excess falls from 19.3\% to 4.0\%, and New York Fed medians also improve. The historical default rule retains FX gains and has zero weekly median excess, but 8 of 60 cases still lose by over ten percent (Section~\ref{sec:v13-rp}). The rule keeps corrected historical headroom unless a selected candidate's validation improvement exceeds one standard error. These comparisons use a separate median for each validation design; the horizon-six New York Fed extension also moves the validation boundary.

\textit{Third, lower scores leave limit-cost rankings sensitive to prices.} Expanded regression is cheaper in 5/34 CFTC cases at the grid center, and in 32/34 or 1/34 at opposite corners (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). Higher reserve charges and lower fixed breach charges generally favor regression, with local exceptions. These illustrative preferences motivate joint reporting of reserves, breaches and exceedance amounts, while institutional costs remain unmeasured.

Section~\ref{sec:target} defines the response. Section~\ref{sec:results} answers RQ1 and RQ2; Section~\ref{sec:cost} answers RQ3. Elementary floor and endpoint properties support the forecast definitions. The forecasting, comparison and cost-accounting procedures use standard methods.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}

Earlier FX studies forecast signed next-hour position intervals using historical states, calendar information and local prediction methods \cite{wisniewski2020,wisniewski2024}. Their chronological training, calibration and test design reports scores and coverage together, providing a natural comparison for the present forecasting task. Our peak response instead uses a complete future path segment and the current magnitude, producing different labels and limits.


Path-risk forecasts address within-period extremes. MaxVaR studies the value at risk (VaR) of the worst cumulative portfolio return under fixed holdings and diffusion assumptions \cite{boudoukh2004}. The payment-liquidity thesis forecasts conditional maximum deficits from confidential transactions \cite{hoogen2017}. Settlement studies examine tail liquidity needs and collateral requirements \cite{petrunia2019,arjani2020}. Duffie and colleagues study dealer capacity and Treasury-market functioning, including profit and loss (PnL) quantile forecasts \cite{duffie2023}. These responses differ from the sampled absolute-position peak.


Regression quantiles target asymmetric forecast errors directly \cite{koenker1978}, while autoregressive quantiles and quantile forests supply other conditioning structures \cite{engle2004,meinshausen2006}. Consistent scores and elementary-score representations permit comparison across evaluation preferences \cite{gneiting2011,ehm2016}. Table~\ref{tab:literature} locates the present peak response, completed labels and matched historical controls.


Recent financial applications in \emph{Applied Stochastic Models in Business and Industry} study conditional quantiles and changing distributions. Lee and Kim\cite{LeeKim2024ASMBI} develop sequential monitoring procedures for changes in conditional quantiles of heteroscedastic time series, with applications to financial return data. Pagnottoni\cite{Pagnottoni2026ASMBI} studies distributional forecasts of high-frequency Bitcoin returns and volatility, reporting forecast errors that vary across time and quantile levels. Muddana et al.\cite{MuddanaEtAl2025ASMBI} use quantile-regression-derived measures of tail risk and higher moments in recursive out-of-sample forecasts of gold-return volatility. The present study instead evaluates sampled position peaks against matched historical controls.

\section{The forecast target}\label{sec:target}

The forecast time is the record when a limit is issued. The target is the largest sampled absolute position from forecast time through the stated horizon, with \(N_t\) denoting signed position and \(\mathcal F_t\) the issue-time information. The peak \(Y_{t,H}\), observed floor \(B_t\) and nonnegative headroom \(Z_{t,H}\) satisfy the following identities.


\[
Y_{t,H}=\max_{0\le h\le H}|N_{t+h}|,\qquad B_t=|N_t|,\qquad Z_{t,H}=Y_{t,H}-B_t\ge0.
\]

A feasible limit covers the current observed exposure, but Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows that endpoint containment can still conceal an earlier breach. We score reports with pinball loss and count a strict breach when the peak exceeds the limit.


\[
\rho_\tau(u)=\tau u_+ +(1-\tau)(-u)_+,\qquad I_{t,H}=\mathbf 1\{Y_{t,H}>C_{t,H}\}.
\]

\refstepcounter{proposition}\noindent\textbf{Proposition~\theproposition{} (known-floor projection).}\label{prop:floor} Suppose \(Y\ge B\) almost surely and the issue information contains \(B\). Projecting any forecast \(c\) to \(\max\{B,c\}\) weakly improves pinball loss for every outcome, with the gain shown below. The true conditional lower inverse quantile at level \(1-\alpha\) has strict exceedance probability at most \(\alpha\), including at atoms.


\[
\rho_\tau(Y-c)-\rho_\tau(Y-\max\{B,c\})=\tau(B-c)_+,\qquad \Pr(Y>q\mid\mathcal F_t)\le\alpha.
\]

\refstepcounter{proposition}\noindent\textbf{Proposition~\theproposition{} (classical endpoint-to-peak bound).}\label{prop:levy} Let future real increments be independent and individually symmetric about zero, with partial sums \(S_h\). Fix a starting position \(x\), set \(B=|x|\), and let \(q\) be the population \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of \(|S_H|\). The triangle inequality and the classical L\'evy maximal inequality imply the bound below. A conditional application requires these assumptions and the endpoint quantile under the law given issue-time information.


\[
\Pr\!\left(\max_{0\le h\le H}|x+S_h|>B+q\right)\le\Pr\!\left(\max_h|S_h|>q\right)\le2\Pr(|S_H|>q)\le\alpha.
\]

Supplement Section S1 gives the proofs and empirical qualifications; Section S4 distinguishes mean-reverting levels from serially correlated increments.


Separately fitted horizons can violate peak-quantile ordering. Supplement Section S2 explains these checks, with complete crossing diagnostics in the extended results.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

The FX application uses the hourly Net Position Time Series Dataset, reporting market-maker client-flow positions \cite{dataset,wisniewski2020}. Its 18,576 records retain source US dollar (USD) units; the later, earlier and full-prefix comparisons use distinct chronological issue grids with the same sampled-peak definition.

The weekly data are New York Fed Primary Dealer Statistics \cite{nyfeddata}. Reporting definitions follow the government-securities dealer framework and position-accounting instructions \cite{fr2004_2013,fr2004_2024}. The Treasury-bill series has 706 economic-date observations in USD millions, measuring fair-value positions aggregated as long-minus-short totals. Additional categories pass metadata and calendar checks and use complete weekly grids, with separate fits by category.

Daily aggregation takes the last recorded position on each nonempty day in the recorded calendar. It retains partial boundary days, yielding 930 days and 501 common evaluation issues, with the completed day's end serving as the issue clock. The resulting target is a peak over subsequent daily records.

The CFTC application retrospectively evaluates forecasts for a set of contracts with uninterrupted reporting histories using downloaded economic dates. It uses futures-only Dealer/Intermediary long positions minus short positions \cite{cftcTFF}. The fixed screen requires uninterrupted report weeks from 2010, stable literal codes, exchange and units. It excludes documented consolidation-code families before ranking eligible contracts by full-sample mean open interest. Seventeen contracts pass the screen, below the planned maximum of twenty. Supplement Section S3 lists every selected code and explains the eligibility rules; complete code-level exclusions remain linked there.

There are 877 weekly positions through 20 October 2026. Validation uses 52 origins; evaluation uses the last 260 eligible origins with horizon six. The evaluation position dates run from 21 September 2021 to 8 September 2026, with the final peak label completing on 20 October 2026. Refits occur every thirteen records with histories of 52 and 260, using downloaded economic dates; first-release vintages remain unobserved. Positions usually refer to Tuesday and are released Friday, so the recorded position clock differs from the public information clock.

Table~\ref{tab:v12-data} describes the aligned source records and complete six-record peaks, with monetary series expressed in USD millions. Panel summaries take nine separate medians of within-series statistics, preserving the heterogeneous exposure units across categories and contracts. Within each forecasting comparison, every method uses identical peak labels, current floors and forecast times. The same-record FX aggregation holds dealer identity fixed while changing its measurement clock and sampled response.

\section{Forecasting methods}\label{sec:methods}

Unless otherwise stated, forecasts target the conditional quantile at \(\tau=19/20\). The horizons \(H\in\{6,24\}\) count subsequent source records, and each peak includes the issue position. Calendar gaps extend an FX horizon's elapsed span, while weekly and daily steps represent different review intervals under the same nineteen-to-one error-weight preference.

Historical headroom adds a corrected empirical quantile of completed headrooms to the observed magnitude:
\[
F_{t,H}=B_t+Q_j\{Z_{i,H}:i\in\mathcal T_r\},\qquad
j=\lceil(m+1)\tau\rceil,\quad m=|\mathcal T_r|.
\]
Here \(Q_j\) is the \(j\)th order statistic, and the training set contains only labels completed by refit time \(r\).
At the sensitivity level where this rank exceeds the sample size, the rank is capped at \(m\), producing the historical maximum.
This finite-sample reporting convention supplies no coverage guarantee under the observed dependence. Translated paths reanchor historical signed paths, while historical-peak quantiles are projected to the observed floor. The endpoint plug-in uses an absolute-displacement quantile; Table~\ref{tab:methods} gives the executed menus and schedules.

Ridge quantile regression links completed headroom labels to issue-known position states and calendar variables. Full regression uses 33 predictors plus an unpenalized intercept, whereas its four-feature variant uses magnitude, signed level and two root mean square (RMS) increment measures. Standardization uses only training rows with completed labels. The initial panel expansion uses five finite penalties; later decomposition and validation checks also include the intercept-only limit in this objective:
\[
(\hat a,\hat b)=\arg\min_{a,b}\left\{
\frac1m\sum_{i\in\mathcal T_r}\rho_\tau\!\left(\frac{Z_{i,H}}s-a-x_i^\top b\right)
+\frac\lambda2\|b\|_2^2\right\},\qquad
\lambda\in\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1,10,\infty\}.
\]
The intercept is unpenalized; training rows alone determine the feature transform and response scale \(s\).
Reports are \(B_t+s\max\{0,\hat a+x_t^\top\hat b\}\). The infinite-penalty candidate fixes zero slopes and chooses the lower ordinary empirical headroom quantile, also fixing the choice when intercept minimizers are nonunique.
The original menus and scale definitions remain in Table~\ref{tab:methods} and Supplement Section S2.

Local quantiles use the k nearest historical states (KNN), with an increment-scaled variant. Filtered paths simulate positions from empirical innovations standardized by generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) and drawn independently with replacement \cite{hull1998,barone1999}. Quantile forests use leaf distributions; tail methods fit volatility-scaled headroom \cite{meinshausen2006,mcneilfrey2000}. Supplement Sections S2, S5 and S6 describe these benchmarks; the extended results retain every result and failure.

Further comparisons apply delayed calibration, fixed combinations and conservative selection to the same peak response. A headroom conditional autoregressive value at risk (CAViaR) recursion and calibration wrappers follow existing methods \cite{engle2004,gibbs2021}. Calibration parameters update after outcomes mature. Fixed mean and median combinations use predefined members. Earlier conservative selection favors the strongest shrinkage whose validation loss is within one paired heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard error of the winner's loss. This adapts one-standard-error selection \cite{hastie2009} to paired forecast losses. The historical default rule uses a separate evidence gate against historical headroom (Section~\ref{sec:v13-rp}).

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:evaluation}

At refit time \(r\), only labels satisfying \(i+H\le r\) are eligible; predictors use issue-known observations and training transforms remain fixed until the next refit. Original comparisons select parameters in the preceding validation segment, while the full-prefix backcast holds its specified penalties fixed. Original weekly comparisons use 52 outer validation origins with overlapping labels.

Matched comparisons hold the completed-response history and refit schedule fixed for regression and historical headroom. The main FX reference uses a shorter rolling history, whereas the main Treasury reference shares regression's longer history and refit schedule. Tables~\ref{tab:fx} and~\ref{tab:weekly} report both alongside the other comparison sets.

Accuracy uses mean pinball loss and breaches are strict peak exceedances, scoring passive reports along the observed position paths. Paired loss differences use HAC covariance with the horizon-based bandwidth rule \cite{dm1995,nw1987}. Tables identify the small-sample adjustment and named multiplicity families \cite{hln1997,holm1979}. Hit diagnostics use a fixed spaced grid and condition the transition likelihood on its initial hit \cite{kupiec1995,christ1998}.
For paired loss differences \(d_t\), the comparison statistic and its existing bandwidth are
\[
\mathrm{DM}=\frac{\sqrt n\,\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat\Omega_K}},\qquad
K=\min\{n-1,\max[H,\lfloor4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor]\},
\]
where \(\widehat\Omega_K\) is the Newey--West long-run variance estimate with Bartlett weights.


\subsection{Later comparisons retain their retrospective status}

The original protocol fixes the two main comparisons and their reference procedures; the original FX evaluation period had already been inspected. Later analyses retain prewritten thresholds and the available local and public timing records (Supplement Sections S10--S12). Original design-object availability is reported by round. These later checks remain retrospective, and their descriptive fractions do not treat related markets as independent replications.

\subsection{Confidence sets can retain several methods}

We form a 90\% model confidence set separately for each complete source, period, horizon and history grid \cite{mcs2011}. The range statistic uses 1,000 stationary-bootstrap replications in arch 8.0.0, with block length $\max\{H,\lceil n^{1/3}\rceil\}$. All complete saved procedures enter on matching origin grids and targets, while incomplete prior methods remain explicitly unavailable. The nominal level presumes suitable loss dependence; it does not account for earlier research-stage model development.

Exact duplicate loss columns enter the bootstrap once, and their procedure labels are restored afterward. Historical reports are merged only after their full forecasts and targets match, while rolling references remain distinct. Several methods can produce identical losses, so one surviving loss pattern need not mean that only one method survives. The fractions summarize related tasks, with no independent cross-market pooled test.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{RQ1: FX gains depend on the historical reference}\label{sec:rq1}

Long-history FX regression beats matched historical headroom in both periods, yet its ranking against the shorter rolling reference reverses in the earlier period (Table~\ref{tab:fx}). This distinction makes the reference procedure part of the substantive finding, since a conditioning gain alone does not determine the overall ranking.

Beyond the matched long-history case, short-history FX regression performs poorly at the longer horizon. Loss rises by 31.5\% against matched historical headroom. Regression records 924 breaches against 427 for the reference; the full horizon map retains the other adverse comparisons. This adverse case shows why the matched long-history gain cannot be generalized across horizons and training-window lengths.

Delayed calibration retains the later matched FX gain, although its breach count remains slightly higher than wrapped historical headroom on common origins (Table~\ref{tab:fx}). Calibration therefore changes breach frequency, while the distinct score and hit rankings persist on the common comparison grid.

Supplement Sections S5 and S11 describe the additional FX comparisons and numerical limitations; the extended results preserve their complete tables.

\paragraph{A fixed CFTC history-length extension.}\label{par:wp2-history}
A fixed extension, committed locally before calculation and uploaded publicly afterward, examines the short-history pattern across 17 CFTC aggregate dealer-position markets. Its fixed horizon is six weeks. The 52- and 260-week results were already known and are reused unchanged; only the 520-week forecasts were new. Both prewritten low-history screens pass: QR4 loses to its matched rolling reference in 15 of 17 cells and QR33 in 29 of 34. \subsection{RQ2: Historical rules lead most daily and weekly comparisons}

The reference-sensitive hourly results motivate comparisons with other measurement clocks and reporting systems, each retaining matched histories and completed peak labels.

At weekly frequency, the Treasury-bill comparison favors historical headroom on both accuracy and breaches, extending the adverse ranking beyond the hourly examples. The earlier short-window version retains its separate adverse result because it uses a different history (Table~\ref{tab:weekly}).

Stronger regularization narrows weekly panel losses but leaves historical rules ahead, with conservative selection choosing the intercept-only report in 17 of 26 earlier cells (Table~\ref{tab:weekly}). Its largest relative loss increase occurs in the Treasury-bill short-history, 24-step comparison, despite conservative selection.

Neither simpler conditioning nor fixed combinations establish a uniform weekly improvement, reinforcing the need to retain the historical benchmark in each comparison. Supplement Sections S2, S5 and S6 summarize these procedures; complete headroom-recursion and combination losses, including failures, remain in the extended results.

Returning to the same FX record, historical headroom wins seven of eight daily comparisons; the exception uses four predictors, history 260 and horizon 6, reducing loss by 3.0\%. It records 37 breaches versus 30 for matched headroom. Daily aggregation therefore preserves the score--breach disagreement while changing the leading forecast in most comparisons (Supplement Section S5).

\section{RQ3: limit-cost regions}\label{sec:cost}

\[
J_m(\kappa,\beta,c)=\kappa\frac{\overline{C_m-B}}{m_F}
+\beta\,\overline{\mathbf 1\{Y>C_m\}}
+c\frac{\overline{(Y-C_m)_+}}{m_F},\qquad
m_F=\overline{C_F-B}>0,\quad\beta=b/m_F.
\]

For CFTC, higher reserve coefficients and lower fixed-breach coefficients generally favor expanded-menu regression over matched historical headroom, although the grid is not monotone (Figure~\ref{fig:v10-cost}).
At $c=1$, regression is strictly cheaper in $5/34$ comparisons at the center of the sorted grid $(\kappa,\beta)=(.1,.25)$, $32/34$ at $(1,0)$, and $1/34$ at the opposite corner $(.01,5)$.
These are illustrative costs, not observed operating costs; Supplement Section S9 gives the normalization, fixed grid and complete-method results.

\section{Implications for model validation}\label{sec:implications}

\subsection{The historical default improves medians but leaves large losses}\label{sec:v13-rp}
The protocol combines the six-point menu in Table~\ref{tab:methods}, longer validation and a corrected-rank historical default. It selects the lowest mean validation loss over 156 weekly observations or 5,760 FX records. The selected candidate replaces historical headroom only when its paired validation improvement strictly exceeds one HAC standard error \cite{nw1987}. Across 60 weekly comparisons, the protocol retains historical headroom in 32 and has 0.00\% median excess, compared with 14.63\% for ordinary regression. This zero median reflects the majority of decisions retaining historical headroom. However, 8 cases lose by over ten percent, exceeding the prewritten five-percent ceiling for such cases; the worst excess is 80.98\% (Figure~\ref{fig:v13-rp}). Both FX periods retain the ordinary regression gains, meeting the criterion for retaining gains. The rule was designed after earlier findings and uses inspected evaluation periods, so this check is retrospective.

\subsection{Validation should report scores, breaches and costs together}


\textbf{Benchmark against historical headroom.} Use the same completed labels, history length and refit schedule, naming the shorter rolling reference separately because its FX ranking changes across periods. This keeps differences in available history distinct from the gains associated with conditioning within a matched comparison.

\textbf{Use historical headroom by default when validation evidence is short.} Longer validation and a paired standard-error gate improve the weekly median, but leave eight large losses in the complete comparison (Section~\ref{sec:v13-rp}). Passing this gate alone therefore fails to establish adequate benchmark protection. The ordinary empirical quantile is up to 36.5\% worse than corrected headroom in the earlier weekly grid, supporting a separately validated historical default.

\textbf{Report breaches with the score.} Show mean underprediction, mean overprediction and strict-breach counts together, since the unwrapped matched FX gain and daily regression exception both accompany more breaches than history. Cost regions add reserve, breach-frequency and exceedance preferences to this joint evaluation.

\FloatBarrier

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The FX evidence comes from one market maker, while the weekly reports combine positions across dealers. Related categories and contracts selected for continuous coverage remain dependent aggregate tasks, not independent dealer replications. Reported net-position peaks leave gross portfolios, hedging arrangements and economic capital requirements unmeasured. Downloaded economic dates omit historical first releases, reporting delays and unrecorded excursions between observations.

The original FX evaluation period was inspected before conditional models were specified, and all earlier-period comparisons are retrospective. The comparisons identify predictive performance, leaving causal effects and the contributions of individual features unresolved. Dependence between observations prevents a coverage guarantee for fitted limits. Choosing among the tested penalties after observing evaluation losses provides a diagnostic comparison, not an implementable selection rule. Validation-length and high-quantile checks were designed after the initial results, with numerical failures retained. Only 6 of 10 high-quantile compatibility fits pass, leaving both FX comparisons unavailable under the rejected repair.

The cost comparisons apply specified reserve, breach and exceedance prices to fixed reported limits. Realized trading returns, funding gains, intervention costs and the effect of imposing those limits on subsequent trading remain unmeasured.

\FloatBarrier

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Historical rules are hard to beat, and short training or validation histories favor them in the weekly comparisons. Matched FX gains depend on the historical reference, answering RQ1 (Table~\ref{tab:fx}). Longer validation narrows weekly median loss gaps, answering RQ2 (Table~\ref{tab:v13-validation}). The tested selection rule has 0.00\% median weekly excess, yet 8 of 60 cases lose by over ten percent, reaching 80.98\% at worst (Section~\ref{sec:v13-rp}). Long histories support the larger FX gains but do not by themselves secure an advantage (Figure~\ref{fig:v11-history}). For RQ3, higher reserve charges and lower fixed breach charges generally favor regression, so cost structure determines the preferred rule (Figure~\ref{fig:v10-cost}).

Use historical headroom when the available validation history is short, and require convincing comparative evidence before adopting conditional forecasts. Compare methods with the same training observations and update times, and identify shorter rolling references separately. Report forecast scores, breach counts and cost profiles together, since a lower score can favor a limit with more breaches. This joint report lets users select limits according to their own reserve and breach charges.
