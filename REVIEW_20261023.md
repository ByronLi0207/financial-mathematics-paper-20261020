# Critical review and manuscript corrections

The 23 October revision makes the paper a clearer retrospective benchmark on a common sampled-peak target. It corrects several interpretive ambiguities and one stale data-availability statement, consolidates execution provenance, and distinguishes the stronger short-horizon FX result from the fragile long-horizon comparison. It does not add a financial model, refit a forecast, change a declared primary or replace an adverse result.

The review baseline is main commit [cbce398](https://github.com/ByronLi0207/financial-mathematics-paper-20261020/commit/cbce3986276e994add53b0f5c6bdeec5191f4412). The complete baseline manuscript and its detailed execution narrative remain in that immutable history. The current [manuscript](manuscript/working-paper/paper.pdf) and [structured source](manuscript/working-paper/paper-content.json) are the revised reading copy.

## Two boundaries that matter for interpretation

The numerical gap concerns the normalized, unprojected training objective. At exact real tau=19/20, an intercept-only sample of nineteen zeros and one M>0 has constant empirical pinball objective for every intercept in [0,M]. Ridge on slopes does not remove that interval. The actual implementation's binary quantile weight produces a very small tilt, so the example must not be used to claim exact nonuniqueness of the accepted floating-point fits. A small objective gap still provides no general bound on the change in an issued report; query features and the response scale matter as well. The frozen initialization and stopping rules remain part of the procedure.

Likewise, reducing both U and V supports every constant nonnegative linear weighting of those two means, rather than every consistent .95 score or every economic cost function. A concrete artificial example uses peaks (2,100), reports A=(2,99) and B=(0,101), with zero floors. Then (U,V) is (0.5,0) for A and (1,0.5) for B. A wins every such constant weighting. A .95-consistent quantile score generated by a strictly increasing function g can reverse that ranking. For example, take g(x)=x+2 clip(x−99,0,1) and S(c,y)=(1{y≤c}−.95)[g(c)−g(y)]: the average scores are 1.425 for A and .975 for B, reversing the ordinary .95 pinball ordering (.475 versus .975). This is the mature generalized quantile-score construction discussed by [Ehm et al., fixed arXiv v2, Section 2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.08195v2), rather than a new scoring theorem. Deterministic issue weights can also reverse the ordering: weights (.01,1) give weighted total ordinary pinball losses .95 for A and .069 for B. This is a boundary of the claim, not an additional financial experiment.

There is an even more direct boundary in the published FX counts. QR has 376 positive-underprediction events, versus 332 or 360 for its two H6 references. The empirical distribution function of its positive-part errors (P−C)₊, including zeros, is therefore lower at zero, ruling out first-order stochastic dominance in the favorable direction. Lower mean U and V can coexist with more breaches; a sufficiently large fixed charge per breach can reverse the linear-mean ranking. This is an empirical distribution statement about these reports, not a population assertion.

## Evidence gaps that remain

The paper still lacks compatible untouched individual-dealer validation, a historical first-release Treasury replay, observed costs/actions linking its reports to a financial decision, and established assumptions for population or conditional-coverage inference. Existing annual groups, sparse grids, alternative histories and model families cannot manufacture these missing observations. The paper can be made clearer and more defensible within its evidence; a stronger method or general financial claim would require genuinely additional research.
